Anders‑Rheinhold Systems (ARS) set up shop in Gagarin Landing a few years before the Colony War.  The workshop routinely signed off everyday repair slips to stay afloat, yet its founders—Dr. Klara Anders and mobility‑specialist Mikhail Rheinhold—were unequivocal about their flagship technology: the Neural‑Interface Conduit (NIC). Most companies of the time found the concept fascinating but ultimately impractical: the NIC captured human intent with near‑perfect fidelity, yet no commercial mech‑control bus could interpret its data stream.  As a result, ARS lived on small experimental contracts and odd‑job maintenance until a more committed backer appeared.  Intended as a commercial gateway into the wider mech‑controls market, the NIC was meant to let an operator guide heavy machinery almost as easily as moving an arm—but real‑world tests never quite reached that benchmark.  The software drew on repurposed firmware Anders had written at Ryujin and anonymised telemetry Rheinhold obtained from Infinity LTD’s covert “Dominion” trials.  Early lab demos confirmed that the NIC could map every nuance of an operator’s movement, but larger mech platforms stalled or drifted—the translation layer simply didn’t exist yet.

Seeing obvious military value, the United Colonies quietly approached Anders and Rheinhold, folded ARS into a black‑budget initiative, and brought freelance mech architect Viktor Petrović on board as an external contractor.  On paper ARS still logged routine repair tickets and modest turnover, yet in practice those same machines were re‑tooled to mill bespoke armor parts for a purpose no invoice ever mentioned.  These components, stamped “industrial spares,” shipped to a small orbital facility nicknamed The Forge, where the UC oversaw final assembly of a powered combat suit: Project SPEAR (Strategic Protection & Engagement Armor Rig).

A working prototype was nearly ready when the 2311 Armistice outlawed all mech technology.  With a single memo the UC froze SPEAR and sealed The Forge under the label Black‑Vault BV‑17‑β, pending an audit that never arrived.  Bureaucratic triage, rotating personnel, and an “admiral‑level‑clearance” tag made the site easy to ignore.  Technicians visited only often enough to verify life‑support, then logged a green status light and moved on.

ARS closed its books in 2312.  Anders, Rheinhold, and Petrović disappeared from public records—whether into retirement, new aliases, or deeper projects no one can say.  Meanwhile a faint, encrypted beacon still pulses from BV‑17‑β, and somewhere behind its blast doors the lone SPEAR prototype waits for anyone persistent—or reckless—enough to dig it up.
