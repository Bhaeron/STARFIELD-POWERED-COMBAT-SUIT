Anders‑Rheinhold Systems (ARS) established itself in Gagarin Landing a few years before the Colony War. While the workshop survived on routine repair slips, its founders—Dr. Klara Anders and mobility specialist Mikhail Rheinhold—remained unwavering in their focus on one innovation: the Neural‑Interface Conduit (NIC).

Most companies at the time found the concept fascinating but ultimately unworkable. The NIC captured human intent with near-perfect fidelity, but no commercial mech-control bus could decode its data stream. As a result, ARS subsisted on small experimental contracts and odd-job maintenance—until a more committed backer appeared.

The NIC was envisioned as a gateway to the broader mech-controls market, allowing operators to control heavy machinery as intuitively as moving an arm. But real-world tests consistently fell short of that benchmark. The software was built on repurposed firmware Anders had developed at Ryujin, combined with anonymized telemetry Rheinhold had sourced from Infinity LTD’s covert “Dominion” trials. Early lab demos showed promise—the NIC could map every nuance of an operator’s movement—but larger mech platforms stalled or drifted. The translation layer simply didn’t exist yet.

Recognizing its military potential, the United Colonies quietly approached Anders and Rheinhold, absorbed ARS into a black-budget initiative, and brought in freelance mech architect Viktor Petrović as an external contractor. On paper, ARS continued logging routine repair tickets and modest turnover. In practice, the same machines were re-tooled to mill bespoke armor parts for a purpose no invoice ever mentioned. These components, stamped “industrial spares,” were shipped to a classified orbital facility nicknamed The Forge, where the UC oversaw the final assembly of a powered combat suit: Project SPEAR (Strategic Protection & Engagement Armor Rig).

A working prototype was nearly complete when the 2311 Armistice outlawed all mech technology. With a single memo, the UC froze Project SPEAR and sealed The Forge under the designation Black‑Vault BV‑17‑β, pending an audit that never came. Bureaucratic triage, rotating personnel, and an “admiral-level-clearance” tag made the site easy to forget. Technicians visited only often enough to verify life support, check a green status light, and move on.

ARS closed its books in 2312. Anders, Rheinhold, and Petrović vanished from public records—whether into retirement, new identities, or deeper projects, no one can say. Meanwhile, a faint, encrypted beacon still pulses from BV‑17‑β. Somewhere behind its sealed blast doors, the lone SPEAR prototype waits—for anyone persistent enough, or reckless enough, to dig it up.

SHORT VERSION:

Anders‑Rheinhold Systems (ARS) embedded itself in Gagarin Landing several years before the outbreak of the Colony War, pioneering a revolutionary technology known as the Neural‑Interface Conduit (NIC). Originally conceived by integrating repurposed firmware from Ryujin Industries and covertly acquired telemetry from Infinity LTD's dark project "Dominion", the NIC initially found use controlling industrial machinery and experimental mech interfaces.

Recognizing the potential for military application, the United Colonies (UC) approached Anders-Rheinhold to adapt the Conduit into a powered combat suit. This collaboration led to the birth of Project SPEAR (Strategic Protection & Engagement Armor Rig), a black project initiative designed to create the next generation of advanced military grade armor. To counter espionage the company maintained the image of a modest engineering firm in Gagarin Landing, performing routine industrial jobs. In reality, custom armor components were discreetly produced on site and shipped to a remote location for further assembly.

Following the Colony War’s conclusion in 2311, the Armistice banned all mech technologies and forced the UC to officially shut down and seal Project SPEAR. Due to its black project origin and bureaucratic triage the pending audits to confirm compliance with treaty conditions never came. Most directly involved personell rotated out within the next few years. Later oversight committees found that the easiest career move was to simply leave it alone. The attached "admiral-level-clearance” note made that choice easy.

Recently, there have been rumors of a technician who quietly maintained minimal system functions for the facility during past years.
